📰 What's New

Ablation shows the certificate-only configuration achieves 4.90/5 under DeepSeek, while removing certificates causes scores to drop below 1/5. Code and prompts are available on GitHub.
💡 Why It Matters： As LLM agents increasingly propose research directions in high-stakes fields like drug discovery, ensuring their questions are auditable prevents wasted resources on flawed hypotheses. Background
LLM agents are AI systems that autonomously perform complex tasks such as literature review, hypothesis generation, and experiment design. In automated scientific discovery, a key risk is that LLMs produce plausible but untestable or flawed ideas. ‘Auditability’ means a human expert can trace the reasoning behind a proposed question. FirstResearch’s certificate acts as a transparent checklist forcing the agent to explicitly articulate assumptions, mechanisms, and falsification criteria.
Key Details

Step-level rewards provide feedback for intermediate reasoning steps, not just final outcomes, which is critical for training on complex tasks. Existing methods typically rely on external search engines that may be irreproducible and produce sparse rewards, making training difficult. Proof Links Predictive Accuracy to Profit in Prediction Markets

rss ArXiv cs.AI · 2026-07-08 · ⌛ 5 min read
Reason：Theoretical advance resolving a key discrepancy in prediction markets, directly relevant to AI forecasting and market design; high value for practitioners and researchers.
📰 What's New
A paper published on arXiv (2607.06166) establishes a formal equivalence between predictive accuracy and profitability in prediction markets. The authors design a 'proper betting strategy' that, for any strictly proper scoring rule, converts a forecaster's probability estimate into a bet that yields positive expected profit whenever the estimate outperforms the market price, provided sufficient liquidity. In a one-month live deployment on the Kalshi exchange, the strategy delivered an 80.33% return on investment with a Sharpe ratio of 3.35.
💡 Why It Matters： The finding resolves a long-standing discrepancy where informed forecasters on centralized limit order book exchanges often lose money while uninformed strategies profit. It provides a robust path for AI models and human forecasters to monetize accuracy, and gives market designers a principled way to align incentives. This could enhance information aggregation in prediction markets, which are increasingly used for geopolitical event forecasting, corporate decision-making, and policy analysis.
📊 Key Facts: 80.33% 3.35
Background
Prediction markets aggregate information by letting participants trade contracts tied to event outcomes, with prices interpreted as probabilities. A strictly proper scoring rule is a mathematical function that assigns a score to a probabilistic forecast, rewarding maximal expected score only when the forecast matches the true distribution—common examples include the Brier score and logarithmic score. Traditional theory showed that in markets run by automated market makers (AMMs), accurate forecasters automatically profit by moving prices; however, most modern exchanges use central limit order books, where this equivalence breaks. The new paper bridges the gap by designing a trading strategy that works on any market infrastructure.
Key Details
The proper betting strategy depends only on the forecaster's prediction p and the market price q, without needing knowledge of the true probability. The proof hinges on a new decomposition of expected profit that generalizes the classical guarantee for automated market makers (AMMs) and also explains how non-accuracy-based profits arise. Empirically, when tested on thousands of AI-generated forecasts, proper betting was the only strategy that reliably turned accuracy into profit. The study further classifies forecasting personas and tailors the optimal proper strategy to each type.

📰 What's New
Danus is an orchestration system comprising a main agent (Claude Code), multiple parallel worker agents, and a stateless verifier, which uses a shared fact graph as global memory to incrementally build long proofs. It was evaluated on six research-level case studies in algebraic geometry, singularity theory, and combinatorics, and the code is open source on GitHub.
💡 Why It Matters： Danus addresses the challenge of scaling AI mathematical reasoning to long-horizon research problems by organizing intermediate claims and dependencies in a fact graph, enabling reliable parallel proof search and potentially accelerating the resolution of open mathematical problems.
Background
LLM-based reasoning agents have recently tackled research-level mathematics, but organizing intermediate claims during parallel proof search remains a bottleneck. A fact graph is a data structure that represents knowledge as nodes (facts) and edges (dependencies), tracking logical relationships. Danus draws inspiration from formal proof systems like Lean but operates with natural language proofs and an independent verifier to ensure correctness.
Key Details
The architecture features a main agent for planning and coordination, workers that perform proof search in parallel, and a verifier that checks claims before admitting them to the fact graph, which is a directed acyclic graph (DAG). Each verified fact stores its proof and logical dependencies, and the main agent periodically summarizes the proof state and redirects workers.
