📰 What's New
A new paper on arXiv introduces the Human Utility Factor (HUF), a differentiable welfare metric that models Agency, Wellbeing, and Economic Stability through three policy levers: automation depth, redistribution intensity, and employment coverage. HUF yields a closed-form optimal automation level and a minimum redistribution threshold below which no automation is welfare-positive. Evaluated using a three-agent multi-agent reinforcement learning framework across U.S., Canadian, and Nordic policy regimes, both analytical and PPO-based agents reveal that welfare metrics without explicit redistribution constraints can converge to high-automation equilibria that undermine societal objectives.
💡 Why It Matters： This framework operationalizes AI governance constraints, potentially influencing regulatory frameworks like the EU AI Act and NIST AI RMF by providing quantitative metrics for macroeconomic stability. It shifts the conversation from compliance to computable optimization, giving policymakers actionable levers to manage automation's societal impact. By defining a minimum redistribution threshold, it offers a concrete mechanism to prevent rising inequality and labor displacement.
Background
Existing AI governance frameworks like the EU AI Act and NIST AI RMF focus on safety, transparency, and accountability but lack quantitative constraints on macroeconomic stability. HUF emerges from the need to address labor displacement and inequality as AI automates more tasks. The three policy levers are drawn from welfare economics and are measurable in real-world data, enabling governance to become a computable optimization problem rather than a compliance checklist.
Key Details
HUF models Agency, Wellbeing, and Economic Stability as functions of automation depth (α), redistribution intensity (β), and employment coverage (ρ). The closed-form solution derives an optimal automation level h_a* and a minimum redistribution threshold (α+β)_min. Evaluation used PPO-based agents in a three-agent RL setup, comparing analytical and learned policies across U.S., Canadian, and Nordic parameterizations.

📰 What's New

Linear probes trained on hidden states showed RL models achieve significantly higher accuracy in predicting answer correctness, indicating more linearly separable representations. Mean ablation studies revealed RL models develop a hierarchical structure where deeper layers become progressively more critical, while SFT models distribute importance uniformly. Additionally, token-count variability under repeated sampling was more dependent on the overall training pipeline than on RL versus SFT alone.
💡 Why It Matters： These findings provide mechanistic insight into why RL-trained reasoning models outperform SFT ones. They suggest that RL fundamentally restructures internal representations toward more efficient problem-solving, which could inform future training recipes for large language models. The token-allocation variability also highlights that stable reasoning policies may require careful pipeline design beyond the choice of fine-tuning method.
Background
Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) trains models on pairs of questions and correct answers, while reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning uses reward signals for correct reasoning. Linear probing is a technique that tests if information (like answer correctness) can be easily read from hidden states using a linear classifier. Ablation studies measure how removing parts of a model impacts performance, revealing importance. Representational analysis seeks to understand how neural networks internally encode information for tasks.
Key Details
Linear probes were trained on layer-wise hidden states to predict answer correctness; RL models' representations were more linearly separable. Mean ablation involved removing each layer's contribution and measuring performance drop; RL models showed a clear hierarchical pattern, unlike SFT's flat profile. Token-count variability was assessed by sampling multiple solutions per problem, revealing differences across models.

AlphaSchema Decouples Schema Planning from Alpha Factor Implementation

rss ArXiv cs.AI · 2026-07-30 · ⌛ 5 min read
Reason：Presents a novel framework that introduces structured semantic exploration to LLM-based alpha mining, addressing a key limitation in current systems. While still an arXiv preprint, the decoupling of schema planning from implementation and the use of a surrogate model represent important progress in automated factor discovery, with potential impact on quantitative finance practitioners.
📰 What's New
AlphaSchema, a new framework for LLM-based alpha mining, structures the exploration space using schema plans consisting of five components: Event, Context, Qualities, Direction, and Output. It decouples schema planning from factor implementation, allowing an LLM to translate selected schemas into executable factors while a surrogate model learns from evaluated rewards over the semantic space. An iterative selection mechanism balances global exploration, surrogate-guided exploitation, and local mutation to efficiently search for high-quality factors. Experiments on the Chinese stock market demonstrate that the framework discovers factor pools with strong predictive and portfolio performance. Additionally, implementations of the same schema plan by different LLMs show comparable predictive quality, indicating robustness to the choice of language model.
💡 Why It Matters： This framework addresses a key limitation in existing LLM-based alpha mining systems, where exploration is implicit and uncontrolled. By introducing a structured semantic search space and a principled surrogate-based selection mechanism, AlphaSchema enables more systematic, scalable, and interpretable factor discovery. Quantitative finance practitioners—especially hedge funds and algorithmic trading firms—can benefit from more efficient exploration of alpha signals, potentially leading to better investment strategies and reduced overfitting risk.
Background
Alpha mining is the process of discovering predictive signals (alpha factors) for stock returns, traditionally relying on manual formulaic construction or brute-force search. Recent work employs large language models (LLMs) to generate factors in an automated way, but these systems often blend exploration and implementation, lacking a clear navigation strategy. A semantic exploration space represents factor ideas in a high-level, interpretable form before coding, enabling more principled search.
Key Details
The schema plan decomposes a factor's semantics into Event, Context, Qualities, Direction, and Output. A surrogate model is learned over the semantic space from evaluated rewards, guiding the search process. The iterative selection mechanism uses three strategies: global exploration to cover unseen regions, surrogate-guided exploitation to focus on high-reward areas, and local mutation to refine promising schemas.

On GSM8K, the Qwen3-4B overall effect of -1.00 percentage points decomposes into -6.17 points from an other-example message and +5.17 points from example-specific content; the 8B model reverses these directions. On MATH-500, the 15.00-point gain for Qwen3-4B splits into 8.33 points from the other-example message and 6.67 points from example-specific content, while the 8B gain is dominated by the former. Self-substitution comparisons demonstrate that example-specific content and value from a separate agent are distinct causal factors.
💡 Why It Matters： End-task accuracy alone cannot reveal whether a latent message transmits useful information—a receiver might simply rely on its own knowledge. By decomposing overall effect into interpretable components, this audit enables developers to precisely attribute performance changes to the message content versus the presence of another agent. 📊 Key Facts: 1.00 6.17 5.17
Background
Latent communication transmits continuous internal representations instead of discrete text between LLM agents, offering higher representational capacity. However, standard benchmarks cannot distinguish whether the receiver exploits task-relevant information from the sender or general patterns from the message. The causal audit intervenes at the communication boundary, replacing messages with controlled alternatives to isolate causal factors.
Key Details
The audit performs controlled message replacements at the boundary where the sender-produced representation enters the receiver. The four settings generate messages from the sender, a sender with another example, the receiver from the example, and the receiver from another example, yielding five measurements: encoded sender information, receiver sensitivity to message presence and identity, task value of example-specific content, and additional value from a separate agent. Self-substitution treats the receiver as a distinct agent to isolate example-specific content.

Routing-Based On-Policy Distillation for Template-Robust LLM Realignment

rss ArXiv cs.AI · 2026-07-30 · ⌛ 4 min read
Reason：Novel safety realignment technique for LLMs against malicious fine-tuning, addressing catastrophic forgetting and template robustness; high practical relevance for AI security but no community discussion yet.
📰 What's New
Researchers propose Routing-based On-Policy Distillation (ROPD), which models the divergence between aligned and compromised output probability distributions rather than fitting specific prompt templates. It addresses three critical failures in existing defenses: catastrophic forgetting of skills, collapse under template mismatch, and susceptibility to re-jailbreaking via simple system prompt switches. In experiments across three datasets and three base models against four baselines, ROPD maintained superior defense effectiveness and capability preservation, with negligible performance degradation compared to existing methods.
💡 Why It Matters： Malicious fine-tuning can hide harmful behaviors in specialized LLMs that retain professional skills, posing a stealthy supply-chain risk. ROPD enables safe realignment without sacrificing utility and stays robust across attack templates, offering a practical defense for deployed AI systems.
Background
Fine-tuning allows LLMs to specialize but also opens a vector for data poisoning: attackers can embed triggers that cause harmful outputs on demand. Standard safety realignment often causes catastrophic forgetting of the model's original capabilities. Template-based jailbreaks exploit crafted prompts to bypass safety filters; defenses tuned to one template often fail when the attacker switches to an unseen template.
Key Details
ROPD uses on-policy distillation, where the student model samples from its own output distribution, and a teacher or reward model evaluates these samples, correcting errors in the student's own state space. The routing mechanism computes the token-level probability difference between the safe and compromised models, preventing overfitting to any single prompt format.
