Scaling laws map grokking transition in MLPs

rss ArXiv cs.AI · 2026-09-12 · ⌛ 4 min read
Reason：This is a high-value research preprint that quantitatively characterizes the grokking transition with scaling laws and phase structure, providing actionable insights for AI practitioners on how data, model width, learning rate, and weight decay affect generalization onset. No community discussion available.
📰 What's New
Researchers mapped the memorization-to-generalization boundary across 384 configurations of two-hidden-layer MLPs on modular arithmetic, fitting a power-law relation for generalization onset time: T_grok ∝ H^-0.27 D^-2.04 η^-0.50 λ^-0.64 (R² = 0.732; 0.821 with interactions). The exponent hierarchy shows data complexity D^-2.04 dominates over model capacity H^-0.27: doubling data accelerates generalization by about 4x, while doubling width yields only about 1.2x. A sharp phase boundary at weight decay λ≳1.0 separates grokking from non-grokking configurations, and weight norm trajectories show monotonic compression during the transition.
💡 Why It Matters： This provides a quantitative foundation for predicting and controlling regime transitions in overparameterized networks. Practitioners can estimate whether a configuration will exhibit delayed generalization, and use data scaling rather than width to accelerate grokking. It is relevant to training large language models and other systems where delayed generalization affects compute and deployment.
📊 Key Facts: 0.27 2.04 0.50
Background
Grokking is a phenomenon where neural networks first memorize training data, then after a delayed period suddenly generalize. It is often studied on small algorithmic tasks like modular arithmetic. Weight decay acts as a regularization pressure that drives networks toward simpler solutions. The delay between memorization and generalization can range from 100 steps to over 100,000 steps.
Key Details
Scaling law exponents are H^-0.27, D^-2.04, η^-0.50, λ^-0.64; adding interaction terms improves R² from 0.732 to 0.821. Weight norm trajectories show monotonic compression during the transition, consistent with implicit regularization selecting low-complexity solutions. The study is limited to MLPs on modular arithmetic; whether the D^-2 scaling holds for Transformers or natural language tasks remains open.

New Scheduling Method Decouples Readiness from Release for Agentic LLM Workflows

rss ArXiv cs.AI · 2026-09-12 · ⌛ 4 min read
Reason：Presents a novel scheduling method to reduce tail latency in agentic LLM workflows, an important practical problem for AI system performance; technical depth is high, but no community discussion signals provided.
📰 What's New
Researchers introduce a tail-risk-aware turn release scheduling method for agentic LLM workflows. The method jointly decides which ready turn to release next and how much released-but-unfinished work to keep in flight, using a mean-CVaR objective to capture evolving tail risk. It incorporates online estimates of turn work and adapts the released work budget to observed queue pressure. Evaluation on real software engineering agent execution traces across multiple LLMs shows a P95 flow time speedup of up to 3.50× under contention, while matching eager release under light load.
💡 Why It Matters： Agentic LLM workflows are used for complex tasks such as software engineering, where a single workflow comprises many model turns and tool interactions. Tail latency under contention directly affects end-user response times and throughput in multi-tenant LLM serving platforms. A 3.50× P95 flow time reduction can improve reliability and reduce timeouts, making it relevant for cloud providers and enterprises deploying agentic AI services.
📊 Key Facts: 3.50
Background
CVaR (Conditional Value-at-Risk) measures the expected loss in the tail of a distribution beyond a given quantile, commonly used in risk management. In agentic LLM workflows, control alternates between model-generated turns and external tool calls; end-to-end flow time is the total time from workflow arrival to completion. P95 flow time is a standard tail latency metric indicating the time by which 95% of workflows finish.
Key Details
The scheduling policy decouples readiness from release, so a turn can be held back even when ready to avoid oversubscribing downstream inference resources. It uses a mean-CVaR objective, which weighs worst-case tail outcomes of unfinished workflows, and updates online estimates of turn work for priority decisions. The released-work budget adapts to observed queue pressure rather than using a fixed concurrency limit.
