📰 What's New
macOS 13 and later run two undocumented daemon processes that collect data from Contacts, Calendar, Photos, Messages, Mail, and location services to build a queryable personal knowledge graph. The graph is stored as plaintext SQLite in the TCC-protected ~/Library/IntelligencePlatform directory. It contains 181 entity types and 278 predicates, enabling inference of kinship and social relationships. These processes continue even when Apple Intelligence and Siri are disabled.
💡 Why It Matters： Even though the data stays on-device, the lack of disclosure and opt-out means users cannot control which personal relationships are inferred and stored. All macOS users are affected, especially those who believe disabling Apple Intelligence stops such processing. This highlights privacy risks when on-device AI operates without transparency or consent mechanisms.
Background
TCC (Transparency, Consent, and Control) is macOS's permission framework for protecting sensitive data such as contacts and location. Apple Intelligence is Apple's on-device AI system introduced for Macs, and a personal knowledge graph is a structured database of entities and relationships that local language models can query. ~/Library/IntelligencePlatform is a private data store directory in newer macOS versions.
Key Details
The knowledge graph has 181 entity types and 278 predicates and is stored as a plaintext SQLite database under ~/Library/IntelligencePlatform, protected by macOS TCC permissions. A researcher open-sourced iKnow, a dashboard for querying the local database. The daemons run on macOS 13 and later and remain active even after Apple Intelligence and Siri are turned off.

📰 What's New
The U.S. District Court for the Middle District of Tennessee approved the final settlements in the RealPage Inc. Rental Software Antitrust Litigation, closing three years of coordinated cases. Landlords will pay nearly $360 million to eligible renters, and they are barred from providing RealPage with nonpublic pricing data for use in its revenue management system. Eligible renters have until January 29, 2027, to file claims. The court's approval ends the main class settlement, though one legal expert said it is not necessarily the final word in algorithmic pricing litigation.
💡 Why It Matters： The settlement imposes the first major restrictions on using competitor nonpublic data to inform algorithmic rent recommendations, making such data sharing legally risky even without explicit price-fixing agreements. Property managers and software vendors across multifamily housing will need to redesign revenue management workflows to exclude competitively sensitive inputs. The $360 million payout and conduct bans set a precedent that could steer future antitrust enforcement against AI-driven coordination in other markets.
📊 Key Facts: $360
Background
RealPage is a property management software company whose revenue management tools, such as those formerly marketed under the YieldStar brand, recommend rental prices using data from multiple landlords. U.S. antitrust law prohibits information exchanges that facilitate coordinated pricing, even without a formal agreement. In recent years, the Department of Justice and private plaintiffs have argued that pricing algorithms trained on nonpublic competitor data can produce illegal price fixing. This settlement resolves class claims without any admission of liability by the defendants.
Key Details
RealPage's revenue management system aggregates data such as nonpublic lease transactions, occupancy levels, and pricing from participating landlords to generate rent recommendations. Under the settlement, landlords cannot supply that competitively sensitive data to RealPage, and they cannot use the system to incorporate competitors' nonpublic data when setting their own rents. The restrictions apply specifically to inputs into RealPage's price recommendations, not to all property management data sharing.

Background
