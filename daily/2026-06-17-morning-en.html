E^3RL: Erasable RL Breaks Autoregressive Curse with Dynamic Epistemic Entropy

rss ArXiv cs.AI · 2026-06-17 · ⌛ 4 min read
Reason：Proposes a novel RL method to address a fundamental limitation (autoregressive curse) in LLM reasoning, with potential impact on long-horizon logical tasks.
📰 What's New
Researchers introduce E^3RL, a reinforcement learning method that uses dynamic epistemic entropy to detect and correct errors in long-horizon LLM reasoning. By employing segment-level adaptive thresholds and advantage allocation, the model can precisely excise flawed reasoning segments while reusing historical KV cache streams for efficiency. Trained on the DeepMath-103k dataset, E^3RL achieves a 5.349% gain over prior state-of-the-art on the AIME mathematical reasoning benchmark for a 4B-parameter model, and a 6.514% gain for an 8B model.
💡 Why It Matters： This approach directly tackles the 'autoregressive curse,' where early mistakes cascade into total reasoning failure, by giving LLMs a self-correcting capability without external supervision. It promises to improve reliability in long-horizon logical tasks such as theorem proving, strategic planning, and code generation, while its linear memory overhead and improved sample efficiency make it a practical step toward more autonomous AI.
📊 Key Facts: 5.349% 6.514%
Background
LLMs generate text token by token, with each step conditioned on all previous ones—this autoregressive nature makes early small errors propagate and compound, leading to 'autoregressive curse.' Epistemic uncertainty reflects what the model doesn't know due to limited knowledge, distinct from aleatoric uncertainty from data noise. Erasable reinforcement learning refers to methods that allow an agent to selectively forget or revise past experiences, improving robustness and learning efficiency.
Key Details
E^3RL grounds epistemic uncertainty on the model's endogenous autoregressive cross-entropy, eliminating reliance on external signals. It applies segment-level dynamic thresholds to identify erroneous steps and uses advantage allocation to determine where to erase, preserving correct context via KV cache reuse. The design maintains linear memory complexity with respect to sequence length.

Quantifying LLM Reasoning Consistency via Structural Uncertainty

rss ArXiv cs.AI · 2026-06-17 · ⌛ 4 min read
Reason：Novel method for evaluating LLM reasoning consistency via structural uncertainty; addresses an important problem in multi-step reasoning, but is incremental rather than transformative. No user comments provided.
📰 What's New
An arXiv paper (2606.17312) introduces a “structural uncertainty” framework that quantifies LLM reasoning reliability by analyzing the stability of self-preference rankings over generated outputs. Tested on five LLMs across eight benchmarks, it shows that combining this structural signal with traditional answer dispersion improves identification of unreliable instances on logical and mathematical tasks. On factual retrieval, the structural signal collapses, marking a boundary where consistency-based evaluation is uninformative.
💡 Why It Matters： The framework offers a new dimension for assessing multi-step reasoning reliability, complementing answer diversity metrics. It directly impacts trust in LLMs for high-stakes logical tasks and clarifies when consistency checks are meaningful versus when they fail, guiding deployment decisions.
Background
Standard uncertainty estimation for LLM reasoning often relies on output dispersion (e.g., entropy over multiple sampled answers). Structural uncertainty captures the consistency of the model's own preferences when ranking its reasoning paths. Bradley-Terry models are used for pairwise comparisons, and PageRank derives a global ranking from a preference graph.
Key Details
The method asks an LLM to judge pairwise preferences among its own sampled solutions, aggregates them into ranking distributions via Bradley-Terry modeling with PageRank, and decomposes the signal into two entropy-based components: across-trial ranking instability (negatively correlated with correctness) and within-trial candidate ambiguity (positively correlated). Experiments cover models including GPT-4o and Claude 3.5.
