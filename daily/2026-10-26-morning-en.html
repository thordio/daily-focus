Self-Direction Distills Agent Mistakes into Reusable Low-Rank Adapters

rss ArXiv cs.AI · 2026-10-26 · ⌛ 4 min read
Reason：Novel method for agents to persistently learn from mistakes using low-rank adapters, addressing key challenges in continual learning with strong technical depth and potential impact on AI agent development.
📰 What's New
Researchers introduced Self-Direction, a mechanism where an agent that fails a task reflects on its trajectory, extracts a lesson, and immediately distills it into a lightweight low-rank adapter. The original weights stay frozen; each adapter adds about 0.02% of the base model's parameter count. Adapters are stored in a persistent library and automatically routed to related problems later. In experiments with open-weight Qwen models across GAIA, Claw-Eval, and Terminal-Bench 2.1, overall success rates improved by 8.9 to 17.0 percentage points, and reliability of corrections nearly doubled.
💡 Why It Matters： For teams building autonomous agents, this replaces expensive full-parameter fine-tuning and fragile in-context reflections with a low-cost persistent mechanism—each lesson costs only 0.02% of base parameters—making continuous self-improvement practical at scale. It could reduce repeated operational errors in agentic workflows and help open-weight models close the gap with frontier systems.
📊 Key Facts: 0.02% 2.1 8.9
Background
Low-rank adapters (LoRA) are parameter-efficient fine-tuning modules that train a small set of low-rank matrices while keeping the base model frozen. Catastrophic forgetting occurs when fine-tuning on new tasks degrades previously learned capabilities. Traditional agents often use in-context reflections to correct mistakes, but these consume limited context window space and disappear across sessions. Self-Direction persists lessons in a library of adapters, addressing both limitations.
Key Details
Technically, Self-Direction trains low-rank adapters (LoRA) while freezing original weights, avoiding catastrophic forgetting; each adapter adds roughly 0.02% of base parameters. A routing mechanism automatically selects relevant lessons from the persistent library for new tasks. The paper reports that a handful of adapters generalize to dozens of new tasks without additional training, and repeated mistakes across future encounters decrease.

💡 Why It Matters： The work shows optimizer agents can accumulate reusable procedures and executable tools across training runs, reducing manual tuning effort and improving sample efficiency. 📊 Key Facts: 10.48 14.33 5.5

HyScale Speeds Long-Context Training for Learned Sparse Attention by 2.07x

rss ArXiv cs.AI · 2026-10-26 · ⌛ 4 min read
Reason：Presents a significant technical advance for training long-context learned sparse attention models, achieving 2.07x speedup on 1M-token sequences. No comments provided, so discussion quality cannot be assessed.
📰 What's New
HyScale introduces two techniques: HyTopK and selection replay. HyTopK finds each query's exact top-k on the fly without materializing a full score table, while selection replay reuses forward-pass selection indices during recomputation. On 512 H20 GPUs, HyScale achieves a 2.07x speedup over Megatron-LM for training GLM-5.1 on 1M-token sequences. It also speeds up the CSA layers of DeepSeek-V4-Flash and the QSA layers of Qwen3.8-Flash-Next by 2.01x and 1.57x at 1M tokens on 32 H800 GPUs.
💡 Why It Matters： Learned sparse attention has been used in production since DeepSeek-V3.2, but selection becomes the training bottleneck for million-token contexts. By removing this bottleneck, HyScale makes long-context training on 512 GPUs significantly more efficient, cutting compute cost and training time. This could accelerate adoption of learned sparse attention architectures for long-context large language models.
📊 Key Facts: 2.07 5.1 3.8
Background
Learned sparse attention scores every previous token for each query to select the top-k, but then reads only k tokens, making selection itself the bottleneck. Existing systems materialize a full score table and repeat selection during recomputation. HyScale's key insight is that selection output is a set of indices, not activations, enabling a redesigned training pipeline.
Key Details
HyTopK treats the output of selection as a set of indices rather than activations, enabling exact top-k computation without a full score table. Selection replay reuses indices computed in the forward pass during recomputation, avoiding repeated selection. The system also includes holistic end-to-end optimizations for training challenges.

Reason：Introduces a novel benchmark for evaluating coding agents under mid-execution changes, revealing significant performance degradation (e.g., GPT-6 Astra drops from 67.35% to 24.79%). This is important for practitioners and researchers assessing agent robustness in dynamic software development environments.
📰 What's New
Researchers introduced SWE-Shift, a benchmark that evaluates coding agents when code, dependencies, runtime state, or requirements change during execution. The benchmark pairs each unchanged 'Clean' continuation with a matched 'Shifted' continuation that receives a task-relevant update from the same saved history and workspace. SWE-Shift contains 120 scenarios across 12 change families, each evaluated at nine scheduled intervention positions. All 17 evaluated coding-agent systems performed worse on shifted continuations than on clean ones; GPT-6 Astra dropped from 67.35% to 24.79% success, with 20.34% paired success. Tool/session changes and stale evidence produced zero paired success across all systems.
💡 Why It Matters： For practitioners deploying coding agents in continuous integration or long-running development environments, the results show that static-benchmark success does not guarantee robustness to mid-task changes. The universal performance drop across 17 systems indicates current agents often fail to preserve still-valid work, recover state, or refresh stale evidence after an update. This benchmark could push evaluation beyond single snapshot tasks toward dynamic, evolving workspace conditions.
📊 Key Facts: 67.35% 24.79% 20.34%
Background
Coding agents are LLM-based systems that autonomously modify code repositories, and most existing benchmarks assume the task and environment stay fixed during execution. In real software development, dependencies update, requirements change, and runtime state shifts while an agent is still working. SWE-Shift models these dynamics by forking a saved workspace into clean and shifted continuations, allowing controlled comparison of how agents respond to mid-execution updates.
Key Details
The benchmark measures paired success: a system must complete the current task, preserve still-valid work, and satisfy applicable state and evidence checks in the shifted continuation. Each of the 120 scenarios is tested at nine intervention positions, revealing that late-position performance varies by system—some agents degrade further while others improve. Zero paired success for tool/session changes and stale evidence highlights specific failure modes not captured by traditional repository-level benchmarks.

PaperFormalizer introduces an author-in-the-loop workflow, in which the system asks the author to resolve ambiguities rather than guessing.
Key Details
PaperFormalizer runs inside a coding agent such as Claude Code or Codex. It uses Mathlib, optional domain libraries, and the author's personal toolkit for formalization. Each Lean declaration is linked to the statement it formalizes, and the author's toolkit grows as more papers are formalized.

New Hierarchical Framework Separates History-Inducing and Action-Prefix Policies in LLM Agent Distillation

rss ArXiv cs.AI · 2026-10-26 · ⌛ 5 min read
Reason：Introduces a novel hierarchical framework for LLM agent distillation distinguishing policy levels (history-inducing vs. action-prefix-generating). Controlled studies across multiple models and environments provide practical insights for agent training, making this a valuable technical contribution, though not a paradigm shift. No comments provided.
📰 What's New
Researchers introduced a hierarchical agent distillation framework that separates the policy generating interaction histories from the policy generating current action prefixes, enabling explicit control over pairing, mixture proportions, and training schedule. In a three-stage controlled study across three models and four interactive environments, student-induced histories paired with teacher-generated action prefixes provided the strongest standalone training signal. Following the student at both levels performed worst, but fully on-policy data became beneficial when mixed with other configurations. Identical teacher/student proportions at each level could yield substantially different performance depending on pairing, and front-loading teacher-generated contexts while delaying on-policy contexts improved over a static mixture.
💡 Why It Matters： The findings give LLM agent trainers concrete guidance on where, how much, and when distillation should follow the student, moving beyond a binary on-policy/off-policy choice. Distinguishing history-inducing and action-prefix policies lets developers improve sample efficiency and final performance by tuning pairings and schedules, not just overall data ratios. Results across multiple models and environments suggest practical relevance for real-world interactive agent training.
Background
In LLM agent distillation, on-policy data comes from the student's own experience, while off-policy data uses teacher-generated demonstrations. The new work argues this distinction is hierarchical because separate policies can induce an interaction history and generate the current action prefix. Self-distillation refers to training a student from a frozen teacher that has additional task guidance.
Key Details
The study uses self-distillation, where a frozen teacher has access to task guidance unavailable to the student. The framework formalizes two levels of on-policiness: one for the policy inducing an interaction history, and one for the policy generating the token prefix of the current action. It supports explicit control over the pairing, mixture proportions, and training schedule of these two levels.

rss ArXiv cs.AI · 2026-10-26 · ⌛ 4 min read
Reason：Important methodological advance in LLM red-teaming with strong empirical gains (up to 53% ASR@5), relevant for AI security practitioners; no community discussion provided.
📰 What's New
Researchers introduced M^3, an automated red-teaming framework that models multi-turn jailbreaking of large language models as a finite-horizon Markov Decision Process. Across two benchmarks and six target models, M^3 improved Attack Success Rate at 5 turns (ASR@5) by up to 53% over state-of-the-art methods. Even with a lightweight attacker model, M^3 achieved gains up to 26% in ASR@1 and 34% in ASR@5 compared to baselines using higher-capacity attackers.
💡 Why It Matters： This framework provides a principled, scalable method for automated red-teaming and safety evaluation of LLMs, particularly in fully black-box settings where existing heuristics fail. Its consistent performance across risk categories—up to 4.82 improvement in Gini index—means safety teams can audit multi-turn vulnerabilities without relying on predefined taxonomies, reducing blind spots in deployment.
📊 Key Facts: 53% 26% 34%
Background
Jailbreaking refers to adversarial prompting techniques that cause LLMs to bypass safety alignment and produce disallowed content. Multi-turn jailbreaks use successive queries to gradually steer a model past safeguards, making them harder to detect than single-shot attacks. Attack Success Rate (ASR) is the fraction of attack attempts that elicit a harmful response within a given number of turns, such as ASR@5 for five turns. Markov Decision Processes are a mathematical framework for sequential decision-making under uncertainty, used here to optimize attacker strategies.
Key Details
M^3 consists of an attacker LLM that interacts with the target LLM and a state LLM that maintains a compact internal state, with the jailbreak process formalized as a finite-horizon MDP. Compared to baselines, M^3 exhibits steeper risk escalation over conversational turns. Evaluation metrics include ASR@1 and ASR@5 across two benchmarks and six target models.
