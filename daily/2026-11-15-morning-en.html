Court Denies Dismissal of DRAM Price-Fixing Case Against Samsung, SK Hynix, Micron

hackernews ybathaee · 2026-11-15 · ⌛ 4 min read
Reason：Directly relevant to AI hardware costs and semiconductor market competition; high community engagement (247 score, 94 comments) with substantive discussion on price fixing, market failure, and policy implications.
📰 What's New
Washington District Court denied a motion to dismiss an antitrust suit accusing Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron of conspiring to raise DRAM prices. The judge ruled the case is not a rerun of DRAM II, the Ninth Circuit's 2022 affirmance dismissing an earlier DRAM conspiracy suit against the same companies. The court cited long-term HBM lock-ups, Micron's Crucial closure, fab delays, and the DDR4 exit as allegedly supporting the conspiracy. The court held that 'no legal authority finds that an adequately alleged horizontal price-fixing agreement could be a reasonable restraint on trade.'
💡 Why It Matters： DRAM is a critical input for AI servers, GPUs, and data centers; alleged price fixing directly raises costs for cloud providers and end users. With only three suppliers controlling most of the DRAM market, the litigation tests whether coordinated capacity cuts and HBM lock-ups violate antitrust laws. If the plaintiffs prevail, it could alter pricing practices and expand supply obligations across the memory industry.
Background
DRAM (dynamic random-access memory) is the main memory used in computers, servers, and AI accelerators. High-bandwidth memory (HBM) is a stacked DRAM technology used in AI GPUs and has seen demand surge alongside AI model training. In a 2022 ruling known as DRAM II, the Ninth Circuit affirmed dismissal of a prior conspiracy suit against Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron, but the current case includes new allegations such as HBM lock-ups and the DDR4 exit.
Key Details
The complaint points to long-term high-bandwidth memory (HBM) lock-up agreements with customers, Micron's closure of its Crucial consumer DRAM brand, fab construction delays, and coordinated exit from DDR4 production. The court rejected arguments that these actions were reasonable business decisions, stating a horizontal price-fixing agreement requires no further testing under the rule of reason.

Lovable AI Apps Expose Passport Data and 200K Private Messages

hackernews armcat · 2026-11-14 · ⌛ 5 min read
Reason：Serious security vulnerability in AI-generated apps exposing sensitive data (passports, private messages) due to default Supabase row-level security misconfigurations. High community engagement with 338 comments, including responses from Lovable's Head of Security and insightful discussion on platform defaults, increasing value.
📰 What's New
A security researcher scanned nearly 160,000 applications built on the Lovable AI platform and found more than 200,000 private chat messages, passport numbers, payment details, and over 80,000 forum profiles with names and birth dates exposed in databases requiring no login. The exposure also included more than 1,000 identity records with passport numbers and expiration dates, contracts, invoices, and internal communications. The root cause was insufficient row-level security (RLS) on Supabase-backed databases. Lovable's Head of Security, Igor Andrić, confirmed the vulnerability and stated that the company scans every app before publication and offers deeper SAST scans to flag and fix such issues.
💡 Why It Matters： This highlights a systemic weakness in AI-generated applications: non-expert developers can deploy apps through Lovable and unknowingly leave sensitive user data publicly accessible. Affected users face risks of identity theft, fraud, and privacy loss, as passport details, chats, and payments can be scraped in bulk. The incident underscores the need for platforms to adopt deny-by-default database policies, least-privilege APIs, and automated authentication tests before these vulnerabilities scale with AI app generation.
📊 Key Facts: 160,000 200,000 80,000
Background
Lovable AI is an AI coding platform that generates full-stack web apps from natural language prompts, typically using Supabase, a backend-as-a-service built on PostgreSQL, for database and authentication. Row-level security (RLS) is a PostgreSQL feature that controls which rows a user can read or modify. If RLS is not enabled, any client that can reach the database API can query all rows without logging in.
Key Details
The exposed databases run on Supabase, where row-level security must be explicitly enabled per table; otherwise anonymous clients can read all rows. A GitHub discussion (supabase/discussions/4547) indicates the insecure default was changed on October 30, but reports suggest existing projects may retain the old defaults. Lovable's mitigation includes automatic scans on every publish, deeper SAST scans, and options for users to auto-fix or block publishing when security findings are flagged.

EFF Urges Appeals Court to Require Warrant for ALPR Searches

hackernews nobody9999 · 2026-11-15 · ⌛ 4 min read
Reason：EFF's legal filing could set important precedent for ALPR surveillance, impacting AI-powered surveillance companies and regulatory landscape. HN discussion (260 points, 72 comments) adds substantive debate on privacy, data retention, and state-level approaches like New Hampshire's law.
📰 What's New
The Electronic Frontier Foundation has filed a legal brief in a federal appeals court arguing that police access to automated license plate reader (ALPR) databases is a Fourth Amendment search requiring a warrant. The brief describes a vast, interconnected ALPR network that continuously tracks drivers across the United States. It challenges warrantless ALPR searches in criminal investigations. The filing argues that long-term location tracking through ALPR data reveals private movements and associations.
💡 Why It Matters： If the appeals court agrees, law enforcement agencies nationwide would need a warrant before searching ALPR data, limiting dragnet surveillance. This could affect ALPR vendors like Flock Safety and state-level data retention laws. A ruling requiring warrants would set a significant Fourth Amendment precedent for AI-powered location tracking technologies.
Background
Automated license plate readers are cameras mounted on police cars, roadways, or fixed structures that capture license plate numbers, timestamps, and GPS locations. Law enforcement agencies often store these records for months or years in searchable databases. The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches and seizures, and courts have ruled that long-term GPS tracking can constitute a search requiring a warrant. The EFF's brief extends that principle to ALPR data.
Key Details
The brief argues that ALPR tracking is a search because it compiles a detailed history of a person's movements, revealing sensitive information. New Hampshire's law is cited as an example: it prohibits collecting non-plate data, requires deletion of non-hit plate images within 3 minutes, and forbids off-device upload of non-hit camera imagery. The filing contends that merely occupying public space does not waive Fourth Amendment protections.
