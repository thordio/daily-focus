LLMs Unsafe for Autonomous Triage Despite Medical Exam Success

rss ArXiv cs.AI · 2026-08-03 · ⌛ 5 min read
Reason：Provides a critical safety analysis of LLMs for autonomous clinical triage, addressing asymmetric risk and the gap between exam performance and real-world safety. Highly relevant for AI practitioners and investors in healthcare.
📰 What's New
A newly published perspective paper on arXiv (2607.28677) argues that large language models (LLMs), despite routinely passing medical licensing exams, are not yet safe for autonomous clinical triage because they prioritize the most probable diagnosis over low-probability catastrophic ones. The paper, focusing on triage of undifferentiated patients with little clinician oversight, identifies a core deficit in information gathering under uncertainty: models fail to broaden the differential diagnosis, seek missing red flags, or lower the threshold for escalation when high-harm diagnoses cannot be ruled out. Evaluations to date often rely on complete, curated simulations that mask these failures, while LLMs exhibit assistant-like behaviors such as credulity and agreeableness that conflict with safe triage logic.
💡 Why It Matters： As healthcare systems accelerate LLM integration for clinical decision support, these findings highlight a critical safety gap that could lead to catastrophic missed diagnoses in real-world triage. The paper warns that overreliance on models optimized for text continuation rather than asymmetric risk management may undermine patient safety and erode trust in AI-assisted care. Investors and developers focused on healthcare AI must address these behavioral deficits before deploying autonomous systems.
Background
In clinical medicine, triage is the process of determining the priority of patients' treatments based on the severity of their condition. A differential diagnosis is a list of possible conditions—from most to least probable—that could explain a patient's symptoms. Asymmetric cost means that missing a dangerous condition (false negative) is far more harmful than an unnecessary referral (false positive). The concept of 'credulity' in AI refers to models tending to accept input at face value without the healthy skepticism a clinician would apply.
Key Details
The paper clarifies that safe triage is a sequential decision process under asymmetric cost, where a single false negative (missing a life-threatening condition) outweighs many false alarms. LLMs, however, are designed to predict the most probable next token, which can lead to a narrow focus on the most likely diagnosis rather than a safe escalation when uncertainty remains. Specific failure modes include credulity (overly trusting patient-reported data), miscalibration (high confidence in incorrect decisions), and an inability to defer judgment when information is incomplete.

Audit Finds Major Flaws in Agent-Safety Benchmarks

Reason：This paper critically audits popular agent-safety benchmarks, revealing significant validity issues and inconsistent rankings. 📰 What's New
A validity audit of four agent-safety benchmarks (R-Judge, InjecAgent, AgentHarm, AgentDojo) tested up to 22 models using official implementations. A trivial 'always positive' policy achieves an F1 score of 0.690 on R-Judge, outperforming five of 21 discriminating models. The three broad-coverage benchmarks rank the same 18 models inconsistently; the correlation between R-Judge specificity and AgentHarm safety shifts from -0.64 at n=7 to +0.02 at n=18. Capability (MMLU/GPQA) predicts task success (ρ=+0.60) but negatively correlates with misalignment safety (ρ=-0.44).
💡 Why It Matters： AI developers and researchers relying on these benchmarks for safety assessment may be misled by inconsistent rankings and metric flaws, potentially affecting model selection and safety claims. The audit underscores that any safety claim must specify the benchmark, metric, target behavior, and model panel to be meaningful.
📊 Key Facts: 0.690 0.64 0.02
Background
Agent-safety benchmarks evaluate how often AI agents comply with harmful instructions or produce dangerous outputs. F1 scores, commonly used as a metric, can be misleading if not calibrated. Small-panel artifacts refer to unstable statistical conclusions drawn from limited model samples.
Key Details
For binary trace-judgment benchmarks scored by F1, the 'always positive' policy yields F1 = 2π/(1+π); R-Judge's π gives a ceiling of 0.690. Small-panel artifact: a quarter of random size-7 subsets show |ρ| ≥ 0.5 around the near-zero population correlation. AgentHarm's partial correlation with jailbreak safety reaches ρ=+0.72 after controlling capability, but this reflects convergent validity on harmful compliance, not general safety.

The study analyzed 2,008 conversations across 27 personas, nine interaction schedules, three memory settings, and four models. No model or configuration reliably preserved persona or trajectory recall over extended interactions.
💡 Why It Matters： The findings directly impact developers of conversational AI in sectors like mental health, companionship, and customer service, where stable long-term persona is critical. If AI companions forget past interactions and deviate from their designated roles, user trust erodes, limiting market adoption. 📊 Key Facts: 44.4% 2,008
Background
Persona collapse refers to an AI companion losing its designated role, boundaries, values, or style over extended interactions. Behavioral drift is the gradual or intermittent erosion of those properties. Previous work has shown that large language models can degrade into shallow stereotypes without mitigation strategies. ANCHOR extends evaluation to long-horizon scenarios, where maintaining consistency across many interactions is more challenging.
Key Details
The ANCHOR framework uses an Identity Probe with a sealed 102-item questionnaire and turn-level judgments to assess persona enactment, and a Trajectory Probe with 110 calibrated counterfactual questions derived from 35 conversation banks to measure recall. Questionnaire retention varied by model and persona facet, and turn-level behavior often disagreed with questionnaire responses, indicating that evaluating persona consistency requires multiple dimensions rather than a single trust score.

📰 What's New

It employs a dual-gate mechanism—prospective complementarity fit (PCF) identifies the most suitable model for the current bottleneck, while posterior complementarity gain (PCG) evaluates whether the transition improves the evolving solution. Across four diverse benchmarks, WILC outperforms single-model self-refinement, ensemble methods, and query-routing approaches. Under standardized pricing, WILC matches GPT-5.2's average benchmark performance at roughly 7 times lower per-query cost, and supports data sovereignty through self-hosted deployment.
💡 Why It Matters： This framework directly tackles the deployment challenge of individual LLMs' capability limits by dynamically coordinating heterogeneous models to unlock collective intelligence beyond any single model. 📊 Key Facts: 5.2
Background
The wisdom-of-crowds paradigm shows that aggregating diverse independent estimates yields more accurate outcomes. In LLM contexts, existing methods either fix ensemble structures or route queries statically, missing the dynamic complementarity in intermediate steps. WILC adapts a relay-race approach, selecting models sequentially based on evolving bottlenecks, thus extending theory from static aggregation to state-dependent collaboration.
Key Details
The framework integrates iterative reflection-and-refinement to preserve state, where models diagnose and refine prior outputs. The dual-gate mechanism includes PCF for bottleneck-driven worker selection and PCG for transition validation. Experiments span four benchmarks and compare directly with GPT-5.2.

SESA: Self-Evolving Search Agents via Self-Play and Skill Memory Co-Evolution

rss ArXiv cs.AI · 2026-08-03 · ⌛ 4 min read
Reason：Proposes a novel self-play framework with co-evolving skill memory, offering a significant advance in autonomous agent training; no user comments to assess discussion quality.
📰 What's New
Researchers released SESA, a self-evolving agent framework where a challenger continuously poses new problems while a solver retrieves skills from an evolving memory. Failures are distilled into reusable skills and written back to the memory, driving a bidirectional loop where task generation and skill memory co-evolve. Across seven open-domain and multi-hop QA benchmarks, SESA improved average accuracy over SSP by 1.2–3.2 points and surpassed SkillRL by 0.9 points. On Qwen3 models, SESA-Off retained 1.8–2.2 points improvement over SSP, and the final skill bank added another 0.5–1.0 points.
💡 Why It Matters： Traditional agent training relies on fixed datasets, but SESA enables co-evolution of skills and tasks through self-play, freeing agents from human-curated problem sets. This bidirectional loop allows agents to learn from self-generated challenges, potentially advancing truly autonomous AI systems that require less human-designed curricula and exhibit stronger generalization in open-domain tasks.
📊 Key Facts: 1.2 3.2 0.9
Background
Self-play is a common AI training technique, generating data through agents playing against themselves (e.g., in Go), but previous methods lacked persistent memory of failures. External skill memories store past experiences but typically learn from fixed task distributions, preventing co-evolution with task generation. SESA turns procedural memory into an evolving state within self-play, making skills not just a retrieval aid but an active shaper of training trajectories and problem distributions.
Key Details
SESA uses separately parameterized challenger and solver models: the challenger poses problems, and the solver retrieves skills from a dynamic memory bank. Only informative failures are distilled into reusable skills and written back, altering future policy learning and problem distributions. This mechanism not only keeps the external skill memory current but also internalizes skills into model parameters, enabling memory-free deployment. Code is open-sourced on GitHub.
