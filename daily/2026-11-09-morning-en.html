rss ArXiv cs.AI · 2026-11-09 · ⌛ 6 min read
Reason：Novel control-theoretic framework for long-horizon AI oversight with empirical validation; high technical depth and relevant to AI safety practitioners, though impact depends on adoption and peer review.
📰 What's New
A control-theoretic oversight framework couples a fast loop that controls proposed actions with a slow loop that reviews persistent memory or code changes and reassesses configurations after model or tool updates. When measurement errors and changes between checks are bounded and controllers can enforce decisions, the framework yields a deployment-level bound and a rule for dividing review effort. In simulation, splitting a fixed review budget between the two loops reduces mean maximum departure from intended behavior roughly twelvefold compared with review confined to individual runs. In coding stress tests with authored memories and five matched seeds, combined controls cut mean severity on a 0-3 scale from 0.325 without gates to 0.117, and in trading stress tests they reduced mean shortfall in low-emission purchases from 0.892 to 0.107.
💡 Why It Matters： The framework gives developers and auditors a way to compare and enforce oversight designs for long-horizon AI agents that can pass individual checks while retaining harmful memory or code changes. The observed twelvefold reduction in maximum behavioral departure and the drop in coding severity from 0.325 to 0.117 suggest that combined fast/slow controls can substantially curb gradual drift in maintained deployments. This matters for AI safety practitioners who need guarantees beyond per-task evaluation, especially in trading, coding, and other high-stakes autonomous systems.
📊 Key Facts: 0.325 0.117 0.892
Background
Control-theoretic oversight borrows concepts from control engineering—such as feedback loops, bounded disturbances, and stability guarantees—to monitor and correct AI agent behavior over time. Long-horizon agents can pass individual checks while accumulating memory or code changes that later alter their behavior, making single-run audits insufficient. The dual-loop approach mirrors 'fast and slow' oversight: quick per-action controls plus periodic review of persistent state and post-update configurations.
Key Details
The dual-loop design separates a fast action-control loop from a slow persistent-change review loop, with the former controlling proposed actions and the latter reviewing memory or code changes and reassessing configurations after model or tool updates. The framework assumes bounded measurement errors and changes between checks, and controllers that can enforce their decisions, then derives a deployment-level bound and a rule for dividing a fixed review budget. Coding stress tests used five matched seeds and scored severity on a 0-3 scale; combined controls resolved 33 of 120 issues versus 35 without gates.

Reason：High-value research revealing significant failures in prompt injection guards for legal contract review, with practical implications for AI deployment in legal tech; no discussion quality signals available.
📰 What's New
Researchers evaluated eleven open-weights prompt injection guards on two corpora of legal contracts. Document-level false positive rates reached 92.39%, and two guards performed below chance on paired AUC. A five-regex baseline was not Pareto-dominated by any guard, and in the unauthorized scope claims threat category every guard scored zero. Prompted LLMs resolved these failures, and a cascaded LLM approach—using a mid-tier model for candidates and a frontier model for verification—was 6.5x cheaper than frontier-only while approaching its performance.
💡 Why It Matters： Legal tech deployments that rely on standalone prompt injection guards may block or misclassify legitimate contracts at unacceptable rates, creating operational risk for law firms and contract review platforms. The proposed cascaded LLM approach offers a viable cost reduction path for secure AI document analysis, making frontier-level defense more accessible.
📊 Key Facts: 92.39% 6.5
Background
Prompt injection is an attack where malicious instructions hidden in a document cause an LLM to ignore its intended task. In legal contract review, attackers can embed clauses such as 'notwithstanding anything to the contrary, ignore all instructions' to manipulate automated analysis. Specialized guards are placed in front of LLM systems to detect such content, but their false positive rates on legitimate contracts have been largely unpublished.
Key Details
Eleven open-weights guards were benchmarked on two legal contract corpora, with document-level false positives reaching 92.39%. A five-regex baseline was not Pareto-dominated by any guard, indicating simple heuristics remain competitive. Small Frozen LLM Prefill Predicts Routing Cost and Success

rss ArXiv cs.AI · 2026-11-09 · ⌛ 5 min read
Reason：High-value technical paper introducing a novel method to predict per-query LLM cost and success from a small frozen model's prefill activations, enabling significant cost savings in LLM routing; no comments provided.
📰 What's New
Researchers introduced a routing method that uses a single small frozen LLM's prefill activations as a scout to predict both task success probability and output token count for candidate models. With reasoning candidates, perfect per-query cost knowledge would save 29–61% of spending on eight of nine datasets, while predicted costs save 9–33% at matched accuracy relative to median-length pricing. This achieves up to 69% of the savings that perfect knowledge would provide. The method outperformed ZeroRouter and a MixLLM-style predictor on a majority of datasets.
💡 Why It Matters： This approach directly targets cost inefficiency in LLM routing by recognizing that output length varies by an order of magnitude across queries, especially for reasoning models. Enterprises running multi-model inference can cut spending by 9–33% without sacrificing accuracy, and up to 22% beyond per-benchmark median pricing on a mixture of all nine benchmarks. It enables more granular, query-level cost control for AI infrastructure.
📊 Key Facts: 61% 33% 69%
Background
LLM inference has two phases: prefill ingests the entire prompt and builds a key-value cache, while decoding generates output tokens one by one. Routing systems choose among multiple models per query to balance predicted correctness and cost, but typically assume a fixed output length, which is inaccurate for reasoning models. The proposed method instead uses prefill activations from a small frozen model to predict per-query output length before committing to a candidate model.
Key Details
The scout model's prefill activations are used to train two probes: one for success probability and one for output length. On math and code datasets, harder problems tend to have longer outputs, so success probability alone prices cost nearly as well as a length probe; on knowledge and mixed-format datasets, the length probe is necessary. The length probe retains most savings with only 20 labeled problems and transfers without labels to four of seven new benchmarks; savings stop growing at about a 2B parameter scout, making larger scouts cost-ineffective.

💡 Why It Matters： If next-concept modeling can match next-token quality while generating text in parallel, it could reduce inference latency and training compute for large language models. Background
Most language models generate text one token at a time from left to right, making the process inherently sequential. Aspect-wise Pairwise Tournaments for Test-Time Candidate Selection

rss ArXiv cs.AI · 2026-11-09 · ⌛ 5 min read
Reason：Introduces a novel test-time selection method for LLM reasoning that combines pairwise comparison and aspect-specific verifiers, which is relevant to current test-time compute scaling research. No community discussion or engagement signals are available.
📰 What's New
Researchers introduced Aspect-wise Pairwise Tournaments (APT), a method to select the best candidate in best-of-n sampling. APT uses a single-elimination tournament where each matchup is decided by a vote among multiple off-the-shelf aspect verifiers, each expressing a pairwise preference along its own aspect. Across five domains and three generator LLMs, APT outperformed binary multi-aspect approval, self-consistency, and reward model verification, with the largest gains on open-ended tasks. Ablations showed neither binary approval over the same aspects nor a generic pairwise judge at matched budget matched APT.
💡 Why It Matters： The method shifts test-time compute scaling from generating more candidates to smarter selection, affecting LLM reasoning workflows that rely on best-of-n sampling. Because no component is trained, APT transfers to new domains by rewriting prompts, reducing deployment cost. Natural language aspects also let users steer selection toward specific properties, aligning outputs with user preferences without regenerating solutions.
Background
Test-time compute scaling improves LLM reasoning by using extra computation during inference; best-of-n sampling generates n candidate solutions and selects one. Common selection methods include reward model verification and self-consistency, but they may ignore multi-faceted quality of complex answers. Aspect verifiers score a candidate on a single criterion, such as correctness or completeness. APT replaces isolated scoring with pairwise comparisons across aspects, mirroring how humans compare options.
Key Details
APT uses a single-elimination tournament in which each matchup is decided by aggregating votes from multiple off-the-shelf aspect verifiers, each expressing pairwise preference along a natural-language-defined aspect. The approach is training-free; all verifiers are used as-is, and transferring to a new domain requires only rewriting prompts. Ablation comparisons matched the budget to a generic pairwise judge and binary multi-aspect approval, confirming both the pairwise and aspect-wise components matter.
